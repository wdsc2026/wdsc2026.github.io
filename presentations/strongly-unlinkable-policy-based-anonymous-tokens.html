Strongly Unlinkable Policy-based Anonymous Tokens With Hidden Metadata Bit

Anonymous token schemes such as Privacy Pass (Davidson et al., PoPETs 2018) let an issuer grant tokens to an eligible user, for instance one who solved a CAPTCHA, which the user later redeems at a verifier. Tokens must be unforgeable, and issuance and redemption must be unlinkable. A hidden metadata bit, as in the schemes of Kreuter et al. (CRYPTO 2020), Chase et al. (CRYPTO 2023) and Baldimtsi et al. (CCS 2026), lets the issuer flag a user, for instance as suspicious, such that the verifier can read the flag but the user cannot. Policy-based schemes (Faut et al., EuroS&P 2025) make issuance independent of the number of tokens. After a single interaction with the issuer, a user derives tokens locally as long as a policy permits. A policy is an interval of policy elements, for instance a timestamp followed by a counter, so that the interval from 202510101601 to 202510101650 allows 50 tokens within one hour. A token is the value of a verifiable random function (VRF) on a policy element under a certified user key. Since VRF values are unique, the verifier detects double spending by storing all accepted tokens.

In the existing policy-based schemes of Faut et al. and Kretzler et al. (ASIA CCS 2026), the spent policy element travels in the clear so that the verifier can check it. This leaks information. If two tokens on the same element both pass the double-spending check, they come from two different users, since no user can spend an element twice undetected. The smaller the deployment and the policy, the more this reveals. We call schemes that hide the spent element strongly unlinkable. Following Faut et al., we ask whether such a scheme can carry a hidden metadata bit without zero-knowledge proofs during issuance and redemption and without random oracles. We answer this question in the affirmative, at the price of a trusted setup.

We formalize strong unlinkability and give the first construction achieving it. Instead of using range proofs, the issuer signs blinded policy elements with signatures on equivalence classes. Security rests on a new interactive q-type assumption, which we prove hard in the generic group model for adaptively chosen inputs, together with the interactive assumptions of Faut et al. that we use. We embed a hidden metadata bit by instantiating the signatures on equivalence classes of Faut et al. with mercurial signatures (Crites and Lysyanskaya, CT-RSA 2019), which re-randomize verification keys as well as messages. Tokens remain publicly verifiable, and only reading the bit requires the issuer’s secret key. We define one-bit embeddability, which captures that a malicious issuer cannot embed more than one bit, and prove that our construction satisfies it. Together, this yields the first policy-based anonymous token scheme with a hidden metadata bit that uses neither random oracles nor zero-knowledge proofs during issuance and redemption. Proofs of knowledge are needed only when the issuer generates keys. Our implementation over BLS12-381 issues tokens at a server cost that is constant in the number of tokens, at about twice the verification latency of the non-interactive scheme NIAT of Baldimtsi et al.

Prior schemes with a hidden metadata bit (Kreuter et al., Chase et al. and Baldimtsi et al.) are not policy-based and are proven secure in the random oracle model. Apart from a variant of the scheme of Kreuter et al. with a weaker unlinkability guarantee, they also use zero-knowledge proofs during issuance or redemption. The policy-based scheme of Faut et al. avoids both but has no hidden bit, and the independent work of Kretzler et al. adds a hidden bit to policy-based tokens using zero-knowledge proofs and random oracles. Neither of these two policy-based schemes hides the spent element. Our construction combines all of these properties, at the price of a trusted setup that none of these schemes needs.

Our starting point is the group VRF of Faut et al. There, a user obtains, in a single interaction with the issuer, a certificate on its key for the Dodis-Yampolskiy VRF (PKC 2005) under a structure-preserving signature scheme on equivalence classes (Fuchsbauer et al., Journal of Cryptology 2019). A token consists of the VRF value, a proof of correct evaluation and the certificate, which is moved together with the key to a fresh representative of its equivalence class. Re-randomization makes tokens unlinkable, and uniqueness of the VRF gives rate limiting. We call the certified key pair a pre-token.

Mercurial signatures can also re-randomize key pairs within their class and adapt signatures to the converted key, which makes a hidden bit almost immediate. The issuer generates two mercurial signature key pairs whose public keys, each of the form (g2^u, g2^v), lie in distinct classes. To embed a bit b, it signs the user key under the secret key for b, converted by a fresh random factor, so that the certificate verifies under a random representative of the corresponding class. By public key class-hiding, random representatives of the two classes are indistinguishable, so the user does not learn b. The issuer key contained in a token is again a random representative of this class, and the class depends only on the ratio v/u of the exponents. The issuer therefore stores this ratio for both keys and reads the bit by testing whether the first component of the key in a token, raised to the stored ratio, equals the second component. Without the ratios, deciding the class is a DDH problem in G2, so verifying a token needs only public information.

Class-hiding also hides how many issuer keys exist, so an issuer could single out a user with a third key. A trusted third party (TTP) therefore checks proofs of knowledge of the two issuer secret keys, certifies exactly these two keys, again with mercurial signatures, and then deletes its secret key. The issuer moves the TTP certificate to the same representative of its key under which the user certificate verifies, which yields a two-link certificate chain. A user who verifies this chain against the public key of the TTP knows that at most one bit is embedded.

Re-randomization alone does not hide the policy element x, since given g1^r and g1^(rx) one can test all candidates. We therefore add a group element h = g1^gamma to the public parameters, whose exponent gamma is known to no party. For every policy, the issuer generates a fresh key of a signature scheme on equivalence classes and certifies each element x once for all users as the pair (h^x, g1). A user key has the form (g1, h^sk). To spend x, the user moves the issuer key in its chain to a fresh representative and raises both its key and the certified element to the same fresh random exponent r, which gives (g1^r, h^(r sk)) and (h^(rx), g1^r). It computes the token y = e(g1, g2)^(1/(x + sk)) and the proof pi = g2^(1/(r(x + sk))), and it sends y together with pi, the randomized key, the randomized element and the adapted certificates. The verifier checks the certificates under the keys of the TTP and of the current policy and checks that key and element contain the same g1^r. It then multiplies the received elements h^(rx) and h^(r sk) into A = h^(r(x + sk)) and checks that e(A, pi) = e(h, g2) and e(g1^r, pi) = y.

The verifier never learns x. A token reveals h^(rx) and g1^r but never h^r, so testing a candidate element amounts to deciding a DDH-style instance, and not even the issuer can tell which element a user spent. The check that key and element contain the same g1^r forces a common randomizer. Otherwise a user could shift the element relative to its key and evaluate the VRF on an input outside the policy. Since y is determined by the user key and the element, storing y still detects double spending.

We prove security against malicious issuers and malicious users. Unforgeability is a one-more-token notion. Colluding users obtain at most as many accepted tokens as they hold pre-tokens times the number of policy elements, also across policy updates. Informally, a scheme is strongly unlinkable if no efficient malicious issuer can tell from which of two honestly obtained pre-tokens with the same bit a token was derived. Tokens are derived on unspent elements of a policy that the issuer committed to, and the same element may be spent under both pre-tokens. The unlinkability notion of Faut et al. only covers users who spend different elements. Since our challenge element is drawn among the elements not yet spent under the challenge pre-token, any scheme that reveals the spent element fails our experiment.

We show that our construction is unforgeable, strongly unlinkable and one-bit embeddable and that it has the private metadata bit property of Kreuter et al. in the stronger sense of Chase et al., which hides the bit from users even if they learn how verifiers treat their tokens. Its VRF is pseudorandom even against the issuer that certified the user key, and it is uniquely provable even for maliciously generated keys. Unique provability holds unconditionally. Besides standard properties of the proof system and the signature schemes, pseudorandomness relies on the one-more bilinear DDH/DDHI assumption of Faut et al., and strong unlinkability relies on their one-more bilinear 2-DDH/DDHI assumption and on our new inverted one-more bilinear DDH/DDHI assumption.

Our new assumption fixes a secret VRF key alpha. Given h and h^alpha, the adversary queries an oracle on inputs of its choice and receives for each a tuple consisting of a randomized public key, the blinded input and a VRF proof. It is hard to distinguish such a tuple on a challenge input of the adversary’s choice, which it never submits to the oracle, from a well-formed tuple on a uniformly random input. The assumption thus combines pseudorandomness of the Dodis-Yampolskiy VRF with hiding its input behind gamma. The generic hardness theorem of Faut et al. for generalized bilinear decisional problems covers only inputs fixed in advance. Building on their notion of non-triviality, we show that any generic algorithm making t group oracle queries and Q queries to this oracle, with all inputs chosen adaptively, has advantage at most ((2Q + 5)(t + 4Q + 9)^2 + 2Q + 2) / (2(p - 1)), where p is the group order. The same bound holds for the interactive assumptions of Faut et al. that we use, of which they prove only a non-interactive variant generically hard.

A trusted setup generates h and the common reference string of the proof system once, and the TTP key pair once per rotation of the issuer keys. Neither the setup nor the TTP takes part per user or per token. The TTP only verifies two proofs of knowledge, issues two signatures and deletes its key, which makes a realization by multi-party computation or in a trusted execution environment practical. If gamma leaks, unlinkability falls back to that of Faut et al. and Kretzler et al. A leaked TTP key allows embedding more bits and forging tokens, although reading the bit fails on forged tokens. A known trapdoor of the common reference string reveals the issuer and policy keys, which breaks the private metadata bit and unforgeability. All users must obtain the same policy, since an issuer serving different policies could partition users by policy key. Where no party can be trusted even once, an OR proof in place of the TTP, as in Kretzler et al., remains preferable, at the cost of reintroducing a proof system and, through Fiat-Shamir, the random oracle.

We implemented our scheme in C++23 over BLS12-381 with the RELIC toolkit and compared it with NIAT of Baldimtsi et al. and with a baseline that adds our hidden bit to the scheme of Faut et al. but sends the policy element in the clear. Issuance is where we gain. The server needs 2.7 ms and sends 661 bytes independently of the number of tokens, against 69 ms and 13.4 kB for 25 tokens in NIAT, and almost all of its work is precomputable. Clients spend about as long as in NIAT, 64 ms against 67 ms for 25 tokens, but most of our client work runs offline. Verification is where we lose. It takes 14 ms instead of 7 ms, and tokens grow from 293 to 1,535 bytes. Compared with the baseline, strong unlinkability costs 2 ms and 289 bytes per token. By operation counts, our online issuance is about four times cheaper than that of Kretzler et al., while our verification is about two and a half times as costly. This tradeoff pays off in the deployment model of RFC 9576, where few issuance servers handle many requests while verification, which needs only public information, runs on many untrusted servers.

Policy-based anonymous tokens can thus hide the spent policy element and carry a hidden metadata bit without random oracles and without zero-knowledge proofs during issuance and redemption, at the price of a trusted setup and slower verification.
